Good morning, listeners. Today is November 05, 2026.

Overnight, all three major U.S. indices closed higher after Democrats swept both chambers of Congress.
The Dow led the charge, surging roughly three percent on a powerful rotation into cyclicals.
The S&P 500 gapped higher, filled last Friday's gap, and reclaimed its six-month moving average.
It now sits back inside its 7,600 to 7,800 trading range.
The Nasdaq gained about one and a half percent, but faded more than 300 points off the high.
The Nasdaq 100 tagged 28,900, former support now turned hard resistance, and sellers stepped in.
Futures first plunged about 100 points on the results, then bottomed after midnight and reversed.
The VIX collapsed from 23 to around 16 as institutions dumped their election hedges.

The 10-year Treasury yield fell about seven basis points, to roughly 4.89 percent.
A Democratic Congress blocks new fiscal expansion, easing the outlook for Treasury supply.
The dollar posted its steepest one-day drop in a month. Spot gold reclaimed 4,000 dollars.
West Texas crude crashed more than eight percent, breaking below 90 dollars a barrel.
President Trump signaled large-scale strikes on Iran may no longer be needed, but kept military options open.
Cheaper oil eases inflation, lifting industrials, financials, consumer names, and defensives.

On the Fed, October ADP payrolls beat expectations with 86,000 new jobs.
Strong hiring gives the Fed room to tighten, and some desks now flag a December hike.
A Democratic Congress also shields Fed independence, limiting White House pressure for cuts.
Markets are no longer pricing a one-way easing cycle.

The bull camp is loud. Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank argue this rally has staying power.
Gridlock means fewer policy shocks, and Trump-era corporate tax cuts likely survive.
History also favors bulls. Stocks usually rise in the twelve months after a midterm.
The skeptics push back hard. Barclays says the Nasdaq's near ten percent pullback was never about the election.
The real problems are AI revenue delivery and expensive financing. Neither went away yesterday.
Much of this year's gain is already priced in.
And the debt ceiling looms, likely hitting in the second quarter of next year.
Republicans have roughly eight weeks of unified control left to act. After that, brinkmanship returns.
Yesterday's pop came largely from hedges being unwound, not from fresh conviction.

Beneath the surface, tech split cleanly in two.
The trigger was Texas, home to 27 percent of U.S. data center power capacity.
Its new Democratic governor campaigned on pausing new data center approvals and scrapping tax breaks.
Nearly two in three Americans oppose AI data centers in their own communities.
Anger over noise, water use, and power bills helped flip deep-red districts.

For the cloud giants, a slower buildout means lighter capex and better free cash flow.
Microsoft jumped 6.7 percent to a record high, breaking through a triple top.
It is the only major cloud giant still generating positive free cash flow.
Amazon gained over six percent. Alphabet added 3.2 percent.
Apple surged on bets that slower data center growth will cap memory prices.

For chip suppliers, the same math runs in reverse. Their revenue is the cloud giants' capex.
The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 3.1 percent, to 10,268.
That puts 10,000 in focus, a level with several layers of technical support.
If it holds, this correction is likely over, though a V-shaped rebound is unlikely.
If it breaks, the semiconductor bull trend itself may be reversing.
Nvidia has now fallen five straight weeks. Broadcom and Micron also slid.
Micron's support sits near 740 dollars, then 660.
Chip suppliers in Japan and Korea sold off in sympathy.

The neocloud group took the heaviest damage. CoreWeave fell 9.9 percent.
Its 2031 unsecured bonds traded as low as 79.5 cents on the dollar.
Its 2030 and 2032 notes now yield above 15 percent, deep in distress territory.
Cipher Mining plunged 20 percent. IREN dropped over 11 percent. Nebius lost 6.7 percent.
The buildout supply chain cracked too. Comfort Systems USA sank 15 percent. Caterpillar fell over five percent.
Constellation Energy and Vistra, both heavy in Texas, slid on fears of tighter grid and pricing rules.
The medium-term nuclear case still holds, because both parties support a nuclear revival.
Lockheed Martin fell nearly seven percent on fears of defense budget cuts.
Tesla dropped 3.3 percent and SpaceX 3.9 percent, on political pressure, not fundamentals.

Qualcomm reported after the bell. Revenue of 10.32 billion edged past estimates.
But handset revenue plunged 26 percent, to 5.1 billion dollars. Shares sank after hours.
Money rotating out of chips and memory is healthy. Compute and memory remain the long-term backbone of AI.
